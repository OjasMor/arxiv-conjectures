# Question 2.19 of arXiv:2611.21894: no. A transitive permutation group with no derangement of prime or prime-squared order

Status as of 2026-11-26: an explicit group of degree 2,239,488, found by GPT-6.2 Astra in one attempt and
re-checked by Claude with separate code that tests every relevant group element. A finite object anyone can
verify in ten seconds. Not reviewed by a human mathematician.

## Source

Scott Harper, "Permutation groups, derangements and connections", arXiv:2611.21894 (math.GR, 16 Nov 2026), a
survey based on a 2026 minicourse. A derangement is a permutation with no fixed point. By the theorem of Fein,
Kantor and Schacher every transitive permutation group contains a derangement of prime power order (the proof
uses the classification of finite simple groups). It need not contain one of prime order: such groups are
called elusive, the smallest being M_11 acting on 12 points. The survey then asks:

> Question 2.19. Let G ≤ Sym(n) be transitive with n > 1. Does G have a derangement of prime or prime-squared
> order?

and calls it "a tempting question, which no known example of an elusive group has yet refuted".

## Answer: no

Let N = 2592 = 2^5 · 3^4, V = (Z/N)^2, and A = [[0,1],[1,1]] (the Fibonacci matrix), which has order 432 modulo
N. Let U be the cyclic subgroup of V generated by (72, 0), of order 36, and U_j = A^j U. There are exactly 12
distinct subgroups U_j. Let Ω be the set of all cosets v + U_j; it has 12 · N²/36 = 2,239,488 elements. The
group G = V ⋊ ⟨A⟩ of maps x ↦ A^k x + v, of order N² · 432 = 2,902,376,448, acts on Ω.

- The action is transitive (translations move the cosets of one U_j, and A cycles the twelve U_j) and faithful
  (U_0 ∩ U_1 = 0, so a map fixing every coset fixes every vector).
- |G| has only the prime factors 2 and 3, so an element of prime or prime-squared order satisfies g⁴ = 1 or
  g⁹ = 1. There are 210,713 such elements, and every one of them fixes some coset.
- A^54 has order 8 and fixes no coset. So G has derangements, but none of order 2, 3, 4 or 9.

Why it works, in one paragraph: if g⁴ = 1 or g⁹ = 1 then the linear part A^k has k divisible by 12, so it
preserves every direction U_j. Splitting V into its 2-part and 3-part, the translation part of g is forced into
one of the twelve directions on the component matching the order of g, and on the other component g has a
fixed vector for counting reasons; together these give a fixed coset. Details in `ASTRA_ATTEMPT.md`.

## Verification

- `verify.py` (our code, numpy, about 10 seconds): checks the order of A, the 12 directions, U_0 ∩ U_1 = 0, the
  degree and group order, and then, for each of the 13 possible linear parts, takes every translation v in V
  with g⁴ = 1 or g⁹ = 1 and tests directly whether g fixes a coset. It works over the whole of V and does not
  use the splitting argument above. It also checks that A^54 is a derangement of order 8.
- `ASTRA_ATTEMPT.md`: the model output, with its own shorter verifier and its reproduction of two examples from
  the survey (AGL_1(9) on 12 lines; GL_3(2) on 7 points).
- Not formalized in Lean.
